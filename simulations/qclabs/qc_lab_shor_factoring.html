Number theory · Shor’s algorithm

Factor a number
using repeating powers

Find when the powers of a number first leave a remainder of 1. Then use that repeating pattern to look for factors of your starting number.

Learn the method

Shor’s algorithm turns factoring into a search for a repeating pattern. Here you’ll find that pattern by hand, then use it to find two factors of a number.

What you need to know

  • Composite number. A whole number with a factor bigger than 1 and smaller than itself. For example, 21 is composite because 3 × 7 = 21.
  • Power. The exponent tells you how many times to use the base as a factor. For example, 2^3 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
  • Remainder (mod). b mod N means the remainder when b is divided by N. Since 32 = 21 × 1 + 11, 32 mod 21 = 11. If b is between 0 and N − 1, the remainder is b itself. For example, 8 mod 21 = 8.
  • Greatest common divisor (GCD). The largest whole number that divides both numbers exactly. For example, the common factors of 9 and 21 are 1 and 3, so gcd(9, 21) = 3.

Follow these six steps

  1. Choose a composite number N from 15 to 50. If N is even, use 2 as your first factor and go to step 6. Choose an odd composite number to practice finding a period.
  2. Choose a whole number a between 2 and N − 1. Calculate gcd(a, N). If it is bigger than 1, use it as your first factor and go to step 6. If it is 1, continue.
  3. Find the period r. List the powers a^1, a^2, a^3, …. Calculate a^1 mod N, a^2 mod N, a^3 mod N, and so on. Stop at the first remainder of 1. The exponent of that power is r.
  4. Find the halfway remainder x. If r is odd, return to step 2 and choose a different a. If r is even, look up the remainder at exponent r/2. That gives x = a^(r/2) mod N.
  5. Find a factor. Calculate gcd(x − 1, N) and gcd(x + 1, N). Use an answer greater than 1 and smaller than N. If neither answer works, return to step 2 and choose a different a.
  6. Find the other factor and check. Divide N by your first factor. Multiply the two factors to check that you get N.

Worked examples

Factor 21 with a = 2

gcd(2, 21) = 1, so list the remainders.

2^1 mod 21 = 2
2^2 mod 21 = 4
2^3 mod 21 = 8
2^4 mod 21 = 16
2^5 mod 21 = 11
2^6 mod 21 = 1

The first 1 gives r = 6. Halfway is exponent 3, so x = 8.

gcd(7, 21) = 7
gcd(9, 21) = 3

Divide 21 by 7 to get 3. Check 7 × 3 = 21.

Factor 35 with a = 6

gcd(6, 35) = 1, so list the remainders.

6^1 mod 35 = 6
6^2 mod 35 = 1

The first 1 gives r = 2. Halfway is exponent 1, so x = 6.

gcd(5, 35) = 5
gcd(7, 35) = 7

Divide 35 by 5 to get 7. Check 5 × 7 = 35.

Why the halfway step works

When r is even, squaring the halfway power gives the full power. Since x = a^(r/2) mod N and a^r mod N = 1, we get x^2 mod N = 1.

So N divides x^2 − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). The two GCDs check which factors of N are shared with x − 1 and x + 1.

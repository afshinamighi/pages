What is computing, actually?

Is each of these computing? Vote first, then click to see one answer.

Computing is turning input symbols into output symbols by following rules. The rules themselves are built by composing a few basic steps, and the whole thing serves a goal we care about.

Let the audience argue about the recipe and thermostat. The point: computing is not "doing arithmetic"; it is rule-following over symbols.

The circuit only moves 0s and 1s. We give them meaning: a number, a letter, a colour.

  • Composition without meaning is just shuffling symbols.
  • Every useful computation has a semantic (what the symbols stand for) and a goal (what answer we want).
  • Keep this in mind for quantum: we will design circuits so the answer we want is the one we are likely to read.
Programmers know this as types. The bits 01000001 are "A" only because we said char.

The bit: one of two states

A bit is anything with two clearly different states we can set and read.

  • A light switch: off / on
  • A wire: low voltage / high voltage
  • A tiny magnet: pointing down / up
  • A punch card: no hole / hole

Unit of information: one yes/no answer. Shannon named it in 1948.

n bits: many possible patterns, one at a time

Stress: 3 bits can be in 8 patterns, but the register holds exactly ONE of them right now. Later: 3 qubits carry 8 amplitudes at once. This is the key contrast.

Longer numbers need a carry coming in

Adding 1011 + 0110 column by column, each column adds three bits: A, B and the carry from the column on its right (Cin).

Idea: reuse what we have.

  • Half-adder 1: add A and B.
  • Half-adder 2: add that sum to Cin.
  • Either step may produce a carry; they never both do, so an OR combines them into Cout.
ABCincount of 1sCoutS
The "count of 1s" column makes it obvious: C_out S is just that count written in binary. Ask: why can both half-adders never carry at once?

The full-adder: two half-adders and an OR

Click A, B or Cin.

Show the middle signals: the first sum goes into the second half-adder. Composition again: we built a bigger block out of the block we just made.

Chain four full-adders and you can add numbers

Each carry ripples from right to left. Try 15 + 1 and watch it travel all the way. This "ripple-carry adder" is how early CPUs added.

Bits on top are clickable. The carry chain is also why this design is slow for 64 bits: speed will matter next.

Counting questions instead of steps

Sometimes we only care how many times we must ask a function, not what happens inside it.

  • A password checker that only answers yes or no
  • A paid API: you pay per call
  • A lab experiment: each run costs a day

This number of queries lets us compare a classical and a quantum computer fairly: same box, count the questions.

Deutsch's and Grover's algorithms are both "query" problems. This is the yardstick for the rest of the talk.

Deutsch's problem: is the box constant or balanced?

A black box computes a function f that takes one bit and returns one bit. There are only four such functions.

  • Constant: same output for both inputs
  • Balanced: different outputs for 0 and 1

Question: which kind is in the box? We don't need to know f itself, just its kind.

Same as asking: is a coin fair (two different sides) or fake (two equal sides)? You normally have to look at both sides.

Functionf(0)f(1)Kind
always 000constant
always 111constant
copy x01balanced
flip x10balanced
The smallest possible query problem (Deutsch, 1985). Toy, but it was the first proof that a quantum computer can beat every classical one at something.

    • 
    
  • The last H turns equal signs into 0 and opposite signs into 1.
    •

The speed-up is not "trying both inputs in parallel and reading both answers". You can't read both. It is asking a global question and using interference to read the answer to that question only.

Quantum search in three moves

 

  
1. Spread out
Put the qubits in an even mix: every box equally likely.

  
2. Mark
Ask the box once: it flips the sign of the prize. Invisible if you peeked now.

  
3. Amplify
Flip every value around the average. The marked one grows, the rest shrink. Repeat moves 2 and 3.

 

 

  
Boxes NClassical: average looksQuantum: rounds (≈ 0.785 √N)

 

 
Square root speed-up: big, but not magic. Too many rounds overshoot and the chance drops again.

 
Avoid formulas. "Reflect around the average" is the whole trick: the one below average jumps far above it.

Grover on 5 qubits: all 32 inputs in one register

 

  

  

   
State of the register

   
Quantum parallelism: 5 H gates turn one value into a mix of all 2⁵ = 32 inputs. Five classical bits hold one of the 32. One oracle call then acts on all 32 at once.
The catch: measuring returns only one input. The rounds use interference to make it the right one.

Each cell is one input |q4 q3 q2 q1 q0⟩, with its amplitude bar (blue +, red −) and its chance. Click a cell to choose the marked input.

 
Step 1 is the parallelism moment: one register, 32 inputs, each 3.1%. Step 2: one oracle call touches all 32 but you can't see it, only a sign changed. Then 4 rounds take the marked one to about 99.9%. Measure 20 times to show it. Stress: parallelism alone gives nothing; reading the register gives a random input. Interference is what makes it useful.








 
The magic is four ingredients, used together

 

  
Superposition
A qubit carries amplitudes for 0 and 1 at once. n qubits carry 2ⁿ of them.

  
Interference
Amplitudes have signs, so paths can cancel or add up. This is where speed-ups come from.

  
Entanglement
Qubits can share one joint state: each alone is random, together they agree.

  
Measurement
You only ever read one ordinary bit string. The art is making the right one likely.

All quantum gates are reversible. That is why CNOT keeps its input and why there is no quantum AND that throws information away.

 
Bell pair = H on q0 then CNOT. Tie back: the half-adder used XOR and AND; quantum circuits use reversible versions of such gates.

Shor Factoring Lab

Every whole number splits into prime factors, for example 15 = 3 × 5. For small numbers this is easy, but for numbers with hundreds of digits the best known classical methods take far too long to be practical. That difficulty is what keeps RSA encryption safe. Shor's algorithm turns factoring into a different question. Pick a number a, list a, a², a³, … and keep only the remainder after dividing by N. The list starts repeating, and the length of one repeat is called the period r. Once r is known, a few lines of classical arithmetic give the factors. Finding r is the hard part, and that is the quantum computer's job: it computes the sequence for all 16 values of x at once, and the inverse quantum Fourier transform turns the repetition into sharp peaks that one measurement can read. Its running time grows only polynomially with the number of digits, far slower than the best classical methods. On this page you factor N = 15 with 8 qubits: 4 counting qubits that hold x and 4 work qubits that hold ax mod 15. Apply the steps yourself, step back and forward, watch the state change, and see the classical finish turn the measured value into 3 × 5. The gate-level circuits at the bottom show what the multiplications and the inverse QFT are made of.

How to use this page
  1. Choose a (optional). The default is 7.
  2. Press H on counting once to start.
  3. Press Multiply four times, once per counting qubit. All multiplies does them in one click.
  4. Optional: press Measure work to see the repeating pattern.
  5. Press Inverse QFT.
  6. Press Measure counting.
  7. Read the classical finish to get the factors. If the result is not useful, step ◀ Back and measure again.
  • The highlighted button marked ← next is always the next step.
  • ◀ Back and Forward ▶ (or the ← → keys) step through the gates you applied.
  • Run all plays every step for you. Reset starts over.

    The repeating sequence, for checking

    Inside the controlled multiplications

    Multiplications for a = 7

    • For N = 15, multiplying by 2, 4 or 8 just rotates the 4 work bits, so it is built from controlled swaps.
    • Multiplying by 7, 11, 13 or 14 is a rotation followed by flipping all 4 work bits, done with controlled X gates.
    • Each block only acts when its counting qubit is 1. A ×1 block needs no gates at all.

    Inside the inverse QFT

    Inverse quantum Fourier transform on 4 qubits

    • Swaps first reverse the order of the qubits.
    • Then each qubit gets an H gate, after small controlled phase turns from the qubits handled before it.
    • Together these turn a regular repeating pattern into sharp peaks, a bit like finding the note in a sound wave.
